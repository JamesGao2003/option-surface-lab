01 / ONE-SENTENCE THESIS

The thesis

When the S&P 500 drops hard for a few days while its longer trend is still up, most of the selling comes from people who have to sell and not from people who know something, so the price overshoots, and whoever buys the drop and holds for a few days gets paid for stepping in.

Why I believe it

A fast drop sets off selling that has nothing to do with what stocks are worth. Funds that target a fixed level of risk cut their positions when volatility jumps. Margin calls and stop-losses go off. Option dealers hedge by selling into the fall. None of them are choosing a price, they just need out, so for a few days there are more sellers than the market can absorb and the index trades below where it settles a week later. Someone has to buy from them, and that buyer gets paid, the same way a store that buys used textbooks during finals week pays less than they are worth.

In the course's terms: the risk the market overprices is that a three-day drop keeps going. Most of the time it doesn't, as long as the bigger trend is intact. I take that risk by buying the shares.

What I'm not saying

I'm not saying stocks always come back. In a real bear market dips keep dipping, which is why the rule only buys while the index is above its 200-day average. I'm also not saying I found this. The rule below was published in 2008 by Larry Connors and Cesar Alvarez (StockCharts has a write-up). I used their settings as they are and didn't search for better ones, so everything since 2009 is a test the rule could have failed.

How I trade it

One ETF, SPY. If its 2-day RSI closes below 10 and the close is above the 200-day average, buy at the next open. Sell at the open after the first close back above the 5-day average. No leverage, no shorting, one position at a time. In Sanji's order language that is one market order to buy the underlying and one to sell it.

02 / TIME HORIZON

How long this should last

THE EDGE

Years, but smaller than it used to be

It has shown up in every stretch of the last 33 years, including the years after the rule was published. It got smaller once everyone could read about it, and I expect it to stay at that smaller size as long as there are funds that must sell into a falling market.

ONE TRADE

About three trading days

The market usually fixes the overshoot within a week. The average trade is held for a little over three sessions and the longest took eleven.

JUDGING IT

Slowly

There are only about eight trades a year, so the course will end before I have a real live sample. I won't read anything into my own record until ten live trades have closed, which is more than a year away. The tracker keeps counting either way.

03 / TRACKER

The rule, run every night

Definition. Every night the tracker takes SPY's daily open and close and works out three things: the 2-day RSI, whether the close is above the 200-day average, and whether it is above the 5-day average. From those it says what the rule does tomorrow: buy, sell, hold, or stay out. It then re-runs the rule over every day since 1993 and adds up the trades, open to open, dividends included. If the thesis is right, the trades keep averaging above zero. If the overshoot stops happening, this is where it shows first.

04 / WRONG IF

What would make me drop it

Three numbers, all computed by the same nightly run and shown with tonight's value.

ConditionTonightTrips atHow often beforeAs ofStatus

Why these three

Twenty trades is about two and a half years. If that many in a row add up to nothing, the overshoot has stopped paying, whatever the 33-year average says. The second is about damage: the rule's worst run so far cost about 10%, so losing 15% would mean something has changed and not just a bad patch. The third is my own record. Ten live trades is a small sample, but if they average a loss I'd rather stop and work out why than keep trusting old data.

The switch that isn't a kill

When SPY closes below its 200-day average the rule stops taking new trades. That isn't the thesis failing, it's part of the thesis: I only believe dips bounce while the bigger trend is up. I checked what happens without the switch. The average trade is about the same but the worst one goes from −9.3% to −13.5% and fewer trades win (72% against 77%), so I keep it.

WHAT I TESTED FIRST

Selling Apple puts, and why I dropped it

My first thesis was the one behind my nightly wheel: Apple's options are overpriced, so selling them pays. I pulled six years of real weekly quotes from LSEG to check. At the money it was simply wrong. The put and call together cost 2.57% of the strike a week and Apple then moved 2.60%. The out-of-the-money put my script sells did keep some premium, about 2.6% a year, but with a t-stat of 1.1 and one week in 2025 that took back four years of it.

06 / MACRO ENVIRONMENT

Where this sits right now

The thesis is true for a mechanical reason. A lot of money is run by rules that sell when prices fall or volatility rises: risk-targeting funds, margin limits, stop-losses, dealer hedging. That selling arrives all at once and doesn't care about price. It is there in good economies and bad ones. What the macro picture changes is whether a drop is just that kind of selling or the start of a real repricing.

—

The years this rule lost money were 2007, 2011, 2018 and 2022. Those were the start of the credit crunch, the US downgrade and euro crisis, the year the Fed was raising rates and shrinking its balance sheet, and the fastest rate hikes in decades. In each of them dips kept going because the bad news was real. So the Fed is the macro factor I care most about: dips bounce more reliably when policy is steady or easing, and less when it is tightening. A hawkish surprise is the setup where I'd expect the next few trades to lose.

A long tech selloff is the other one. The biggest weights in the S&P 500 are the large tech companies, so an unwind in those names that lasts weeks would drag the index through its 200-day average and switch the rule off, which is what the filter is for. Rates help in a small way: three-month T-bills pay 4.13%, and the money sits in cash about 90% of the time.

07 / HOW IT STAYS CURRENT

Nightly update

The tracker runs in the same GitHub Action as my order planner, every night at 10 PM New York. build_tracker.py is standard-library Python. It downloads SPY's latest daily bars from Yahoo (Stooq is the backup), adds them to the history stored in data/, re-runs the rule from 1993, and commits tracker.json. This page only reads that file. If the download fails the chart can be a day stale, but it can't go blank.

Data

SPY daily open and close as traded, and its dividends, from Yahoo, back to January 1993. Indicators use the unadjusted close, which is what a trader sees that evening. Trade returns are open to open plus any dividend that went ex while the position was held.

What isn't in it

Fills are assumed at the official opening print, less 5 basis points per round trip. Interest on idle cash is left out of every number on this page. Daily bars can't show how bad a trade looked inside the day. And the rule comes from a book, so the years before 2009 may be flattered by the authors having picked what worked.
