01 / ONE-SENTENCE THESIS

The thesis

Crash protection on Apple is overpriced: the people buying out-of-the-money puts pay more than the drops they are insuring against have cost, and nobody is forced to sell to them, so a cash-secured seller of those puts keeps part of the premium over time.

Why I believe it

Apple is one of the largest holdings in almost every index fund and in a lot of personal accounts. People who own that much of one stock buy puts so a bad month doesn't hurt as much. There's no matching group that has to sell puts, so the price has to be high enough to get someone like me to take the other side, the same way a car insurance premium is higher than the average claim. In the course's terms, the risk I think is overpriced is a sharp drop in Apple over the next week, and I accept that risk by writing the put.

Two things in the data point the same way. Cboe publishes an "Apple VIX" (VXAPL), the volatility that Apple's option prices imply for the next 30 days, counting the far strikes where the protection is bought. Since mid-2010 it was above the volatility Apple then delivered on about three days in four, by about four points on average (section 03). And in six years of real quotes, the put my script sells collected more than it paid back (section 05).

What I'm not saying

I'm not saying every Apple option is overpriced. I checked, and at-the-money options were priced almost exactly right. I'm not saying Apple will go up. And I'm not saying the seller wins every month: when the stock gaps down the seller pays, and one bad week can take back years of premium. The claim is only about the average over many weeks.

How I trade it

Through the nightly wheel on the Final Cycle page. When the book is flat the script sells one cash-secured Apple put for that Friday, 1.3 weekly sigmas below the open, which has averaged 4.6% below the stock. If the put is assigned, it holds the shares and sells a covered call until they are called away.

02 / TIME HORIZON

How long this should last

THE EDGE

Years, with bad stretches inside

This isn't a mispricing the market will notice one day and fix. It's a fee for insurance, and it gets charged again every month, so I don't expect it to close. The yearly average gap has been above zero in all 17 calendar years from 2010 to 2026, though 2018 and 2020 were barely positive. I expect it to keep paying as long as Apple stays a stock that lots of people own and want to protect.

ONE TRADE

About five trading days

Each put is sold at Monday's open and expires that Friday, so the normal trade lasts one week. In the six-year check the put finished in the money 23 times in 305 weeks, about one week in thirteen. When that happens the wheel holds the shares and sells calls until they are called away; in my Amazon wheel backtest that took 38 trading days. Weighted together I expect the average trade to last a bit over one week.

JUDGING IT

Two months minimum, a year to be sure

One month tells me almost nothing. The month-end gap averages +4.4 points with a standard deviation of 9.6, so single months are often negative (51 of 198). I won't read anything into my own record until two option months, 42 sessions, have passed since I wrote this, and the test I trust most uses a full year.

03 / TRACKER

Implied against realised, every day

Definition. For each trading day: take the Apple VIX from 21 sessions earlier (what option prices said Apple's volatility would be over the coming month) and subtract the annualised standard deviation of Apple's daily log returns over those 21 sessions (what it turned out to be). That difference is the settled gap, in volatility points. Positive means option buyers paid for more movement than they got. The Apple VIX is built from the whole range of strikes, including the far out-of-the-money puts, so it carries the price of protection, which is what the thesis is about. I use the settled version, not today's implied minus the last month's realised, because only the settled one compares a forecast with the thing it was forecasting.

04 / WRONG IF

What would make me drop it

Why these three

The first says the premium has been gone for a whole year. That has been true on 5.9% of days since 2011, in three stretches: most of 2019, spring 2020 and spring 2025. The second says the seller has been losing more often than winning for six months, which has been true on 3.9% of days. The third is my own record: if the average gap since 23 November is still zero or negative after two option months, then whatever was true before, it isn't working while I'm trading it.

What I tried first and threw out

My first draft used fast stops: quit when the 60-session average goes negative, or when the Apple VIX closes above 45. Then I checked what happened next. After the 60-session average went negative, the following month's gap averaged +5.3 points against +3.7 the rest of the time. After a close above 45, it averaged +9.0. So the fast stops would have switched me off right when options were most expensive. A bad month here is usually followed by a good one, which is why the tests above only fire on something that lasts.

05 / ROUGH ALPHA AND COST

How much there is to collect

I didn't want to guess this from a pricing model, so I pulled real quotes. For every week since October 2020 (after Apple's last split) I took the closing bid and ask of three weekly Apple options on the first session of the week from LSEG: the put my rule would pick, the at-the-money put, and the at-the-money call. Sell at the bid, hold to expiry, settle against that day's close.

What I take from it

At the money there was nothing to collect. Over 318 weeks the at-the-money put and call together cost 2.57% of the strike and Apple then moved 2.60%. The market charged too much in 61% of weeks, but the weeks it charged too little were bigger, and after the bid-ask spread a seller lost about 4.8% a year. So "Apple options are overpriced" is wrong as a blanket statement, and I rewrote my thesis sentence after seeing this.

The put my script sells is a different story. It collected 0.21% of the strike a week and paid back 0.15%, so the seller kept about a quarter of what came in: +0.05% a week, about +2.6% a year on the cash behind the put, before whatever that cash earns in T-bills. Six of the seven calendar years were positive. My read is that what the tracker measures is the price of crash protection. Cboe's index counts the far strikes and averaged 30.3 over these six years, while the at-the-money weekly options were only implying about 25.6.

How sure, and what it costs

Not very sure. A t-stat of 1.1 over six years isn't proof. One week, 31 March 2025, when Apple fell 15%, cost 10.1% of the strike, which is about four years of average profit, and over the last two years alone the same put lost 2.8% a year. I'd plan on 1 to 2% a year over T-bills, not 2.6.

Costs are small next to that but not nothing. The average bid was $0.38 a share, $38 a contract. Half the bid-ask spread was 1.5 cents, about 4% of the premium, and the $0.65 commission is another 1.7%. Both are already in the table because I sell at the bid. My live order asks for the mid, and if that fills it adds about 0.4% a year. At today's price one put ties up about $33,000, so 2.6% is around $860 a year, or $17 a week, per contract.

06 / MACRO ENVIRONMENT

Where this sits right now

The thesis is true for a boring reason: Apple is in nearly every index fund and a lot of single-stock accounts, the people holding it want protection against a sharp drop, and they pay for it whether or not the drop comes. That demand doesn't depend on the cycle. What the cycle changes is how often the drop does come.

Right now the market is jumpy. The VIX closed at 35.9 on Friday 20 November, its highest close of 2026 and above 98% of all days since 2010, then fell back to 26.6 today. Apple's own VIX went from 27.6 to 34.3 and back to 27.7 over the same three sessions. That is the kind of month that hurts this trade. In months when the VIX rose more than 10 points, the settled gap averaged −13.7 points and was positive only 28% of the time. In months when the VIX drifted lower it averaged +5.9 and was positive 83% of the time. So the main way I lose is a broad selloff in large tech, because Apple falls with the index, and a put 5% below isn't far away in a week like that.

The worst weeks in the six-year check line up with macro and company news, not with anything on a chart: the hot CPI print in the week of 12 September 2022, Apple's earnings in the week of 31 July 2023, and the tariff announcement in the week of 31 March 2025. My script doesn't look at the calendar yet. Apple's next earnings are due around the end of January, and for the final project I plan to skip the put that week or move it further out.

Rates matter in a quieter way. Three-month T-bills pay 4.13%, and the cash that secures the put earns that, which is more than the put itself has paid. If the Fed cuts, less of the total return comes from the cash and more has to come from the premium. A surprise from the Fed in either direction usually shows up first as a jump in implied volatility, which is bad for a put I've already sold and good for the price of the next one.

07 / HOW IT STAYS CURRENT

Nightly update

The tracker runs in the same GitHub Action as the order planner, every night at 10 PM New York. build_tracker.py is standard-library Python: it downloads the Apple VIX history file from Cboe (FRED and Yahoo are the backups), takes Apple's close from the bars the course server pushes into price_history.json, recomputes everything, and commits tracker.json. This page only reads that file. If a download fails, the script keeps the history already stored in data/ and says so in the notes at the bottom of the JSON, so the chart can go stale by a day but can't go blank.

Data

Apple VIX: Cboe's VXAPL index, daily closes from Cboe's own history file, with FRED series VXAPLCLS for 2010 and as a backup. The two agree on every day they share. Apple closes: Interactive Brokers daily bars from the course server since 23 September 2026, Yahoo before that. Option quotes for section 05: LSEG Workspace, daily closing bid and ask of expired weekly contracts from October 2020, pulled once on 23 November 2026. 319 of 320 weeks came back with quotes; the missing one is last week (16 November), which returned nothing.

What isn't in it

The Apple VIX is a 30-day number built from the whole strike range, and I trade a one-week put at one strike, so the tracker measures the belief and not my exact trade. Section 05 checks the trade itself, but that was a one-off pull, not a nightly one. Once Sanji starts filling orders, the book's own profit and loss becomes a fourth wrong-if test. Dividends and early assignment are ignored. The option check uses closing quotes, not the open my live order fills at.
